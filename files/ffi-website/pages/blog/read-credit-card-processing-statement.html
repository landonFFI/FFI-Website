Your statement isn't written for you to understand. Here's how to read it anyway, spot the fees that shouldn't be there, and find out your real cost to take a card.

You take a look at your processing statement every month, see a number, and pay it. You don't really know what half the lines mean. Assessment fee. Batch fee. Non-qualified surcharge. It reads like it was written to be skipped, not read.

That's not an accident. The company holding your statement has no reason to make it easy to catch a fee that shouldn't be there.

You don't need to become a payments expert to fix this. You need two numbers, a few terms, and ten minutes.

This is general information, not legal or accounting advice. Check with your own processor or accountant about the specific fees on your account.

What's actually on a processing statement?

Every statement, no matter who sends it, is built from the same three pieces:

  1. Interchange. A fee set by Visa, Mastercard, and other card networks, paid to the customer's bank. This is the biggest slice, and it's the same no matter which processor you use.
  2. Assessments. A small, fixed fee the card networks charge on every transaction, usually well under half a percent.
  3. Processor markup. Whatever your processor adds on top to make their money. This is the only piece that's actually negotiable.

Interchange and assessments are set by the card networks, according to Visa's and Mastercard's own published rate information. Your processor doesn't control them and can't discount them. What varies wildly from one processor to the next is the markup, and that's usually where the fees you don't recognize come from.

Statements from different processors don't all look alike, either. Some show a single bundled rate. Others break out interchange, assessments, and markup line by line. If yours is bundled into one number, that's not automatically bad, but it makes junk fees easier to hide, since you can't tell which piece went up.

How do you find your real cost?

Skip the fine print for a minute and find two numbers on your statement:

  • Total card sales for the month
  • Total fees charged for the month

Divide fees by sales. That's your effective rate.

For example, if you did $20,000 in card sales and paid $600 in total fees, your effective rate is 3%.

Most small businesses that mostly take chip and tap payments in person land between 2.5% and 3.5%, according to payments industry guidance compiled by Finli. If you're paying more than that and don't process a lot of cards by phone or online, where rates run higher, that gap is worth asking about.

What junk fees should you look for?

Not every fee is a red flag. PCI compliance, a statement fee, and a small monthly minimum are all normal parts of processing. The problem is when they're padded or duplicated. Watch for:

  • A PCI fee far above what compliance actually costs. A little is normal. A lot is markup with a compliance label on it.
  • A "non-qualified" or "mid-qualified" surcharge on cards that should have processed at your normal rate.
  • Multiple small fees that do the same thing, like a "statement fee" and a separate "regulatory fee" that both just mean "we're charging you to send this statement."
  • A rate that jumped with no explanation and no change in how you take payments.

None of these are illegal. They're just fees a processor is allowed to charge, and counting on you not to ask.

The average small business pays somewhere between $3,500 and $5,000 a year in processing fees, according to Finli's guide for small business owners. Most of that is interchange you can't avoid. But if even a few hundred dollars of it is padded markup, it adds up every single month, year after year, for as long as nobody looks.

What do you do if something looks wrong?

Call your processor first and ask them to walk through the fee in plain language. A processor with nothing to hide will explain it. One that won't, or gives an answer that doesn't match your statement, is telling you something.

If the answer still doesn't add up, or you'd rather have someone independent do the math, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau takes complaints about processors and banks, though it's best used for patterns that go beyond your own account. For a straight answer on your own statement, a second set of eyes that isn't trying to sell you a new terminal is usually faster.

That's true whether or not you already have an ATM in your business. Pairing a clear answer on your card processing with your existing cash setup means one local contact for both, instead of guessing at two separate bills every month.

The bottom line

A processing statement looks complicated because it's convenient for it to look that way. It isn't. Two numbers, divided, tell you your real rate. A short list of common junk fees tells you what to question. That's most of what you need to stop paying for things you can't explain.

Interchange is the fee card networks like Visa and Mastercard set for each transaction, and it's paid to the customer's bank. It isn't negotiable. What you can negotiate is your processor's markup on top of it, which is where most of the savings actually are.

Calculate your effective rate and compare it to the 2.5%–3.5% range typical for small businesses. Also check for fees that don't explain themselves and rate increases you were never told about. A sudden jump from one month to the next, with no change in how you take payments, is a clear sign.
