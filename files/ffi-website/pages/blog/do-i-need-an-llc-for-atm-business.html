Starting an ATM Business

Do I Need an LLC for My ATM Business?

No law says you have to form one. But an LLC changes who is on the hook when something goes wrong. Here's what it does, what it costs in Alabama, and when to set it up.

By Landon Ford, owner of Ford Frontier Investments, Birmingham, Alabama.
Updated November 23, 2026. About a 4-minute read.

Do I Need an LLC for My ATM Business?

The short answer

No. You can legally own and run an ATM as a sole proprietor. But an LLC keeps your home, car, and savings apart from the business's debts and lawsuits in most cases. In Alabama, forming one costs about $225 in state fees. Most new operators should set it up before they sign a location agreement.

You've picked a machine. Maybe you've even got a store owner who said yes. Then the paperwork questions start.

Do you need an LLC? Do you need it before you buy? Will the ATM company ask for one?

Ask online and you'll get two answers. Half the internet says "you must." The other half says "don't bother." Both are a little wrong.

Here's the honest answer, with real Alabama costs.

Do you need an LLC to own an ATM?

No. No law says an ATM owner has to form an LLC. If you buy a machine in your own name and start earning surcharge fees, you're a sole proprietor. That's the default for anyone who does business alone.

But "legal" and "smart" aren't the same thing. The question is what happens if something goes wrong.

What does an LLC actually protect?

The U.S. Small Business Administration puts it plainly. As a sole proprietor, the business's debts are your debts. If the business is sued, your personal assets are on the line.

An LLC changes that. The SBA says an LLC protects your personal assets in most cases. If the business gets sued or can't pay a bill, your house, car, and savings usually aren't part of it.

For an ATM owner, think about the things that could go wrong:

  • A customer gets hurt near your machine and sues.
  • A location owner says you broke your agreement.
  • You buy several machines and the business can't cover a bill.

Here's what an LLC does not do:

  • It doesn't protect the business's own money. The machine and the cash inside it belong to the business. If they're stolen, the business takes the loss.
  • It doesn't cover anything you sign personally. If you sign a loan or lease in your own name, or promise to pay it yourself, you're still on the hook.
  • It doesn't replace insurance. The SBA notes that LLC protection has limits.
  • It only works if you keep things separate. Mix your personal and ATM money in one account and you weaken the protection. The SBA also suggests a written operating agreement.

What does an LLC cost in Alabama?

Less than most people expect. Here are the state fees from the Alabama Secretary of State:

Step Fee
Reserve your business name $25 by mail, $28 online
File the Certificate of Formation $200
Total to start About $225 to $228

Paying by card online adds a small fee. Part of the $200 goes to the county where your registered agent is located.

What about yearly costs? Alabama has a business privilege tax. The Alabama Department of Revenue says businesses that would owe $100 or less are fully exempt. For 2026, those small businesses don't have to file the return either. A small ATM business may fall in that range. Your tax preparer can tell you for sure.

You may also need a city or county business license, LLC or not. Birmingham, for example, says anyone doing business inside city limits must register with the city. Rules vary by city, so call the city where each machine sits.

Does an LLC change your taxes?

Not by default. The IRS treats a one-owner LLC as part of its owner for income tax. You report ATM income and costs on Schedule C, the same as a sole proprietor. You can elect a different tax setup later, but that's a talk for your tax preparer.

A one-owner LLC with no employees doesn't always need its own federal tax ID number (an EIN). The IRS says you can still get one for banking reasons, and a bank may ask for one when you open a business account.

This is general information, not legal or tax advice.

When should you set it up?

Before you sign anything for the business. That means before:

  1. You sign your first location agreement.
  2. You set up ATM processing.
  3. You open the bank account where your surcharge gets deposited.

If you sign a location agreement in your own name, you're the one bound by it. Moving it to an LLC later means new paperwork with the store owner, the processor, and the bank. It's easier to start clean.

Getting your deposits right matters too. Read how ATM owners get paid to see where the money goes and when.

Should each ATM get its own LLC?

You'll see this advice online. For most new operators, it's overkill.

Every LLC is its own company. That means its own $225 or so in Alabama fees, its own bank account, and its own records. With one or two machines, one LLC is usually plenty. If you grow into a large route, ask a lawyer whether splitting makes sense for you.

Here's how the two choices compare

Sole proprietor One-owner LLC
Alabama state fees to start $0 About $225
Personal assets protected from business lawsuits No In most cases
Federal income tax by default Schedule C Schedule C
Separate bank account Strongly advised Needed to keep protection
Best fit Testing one machine at a location you control Placing machines in other people's businesses

So, do you need one?

You don't have to have one. But if you're putting machines in other people's businesses, about $225 is a small price for keeping your house out of a lawsuit. Set it up before you sign anything, keep the money separate, and talk to a tax preparer about the details.
