A customer walks up to your counter, annoyed. "Your ATM took $100 out of my account and gave me nothing."

Maybe they're telling the truth. Maybe the machine jammed. Maybe they walked off without grabbing the bills. Maybe they're hoping you'll just hand over cash to make the problem go away.

You can't tell from where you're standing. And you shouldn't have to. There's a process built for exactly this moment, and it doesn't involve your register.

## Whose problem is it when an ATM keeps a customer's money?

Not yours alone, and usually not yours to pay. Three parties are involved.

- **The customer's bank.** It takes the claim and runs the investigation.
- **The owner of the cash in the machine.** It has to prove what the machine did.
- **You.** If you only host the machine, you're the place it sits.

That last point is why your agreement matters. A good one says in writing who is responsible if something goes wrong. If yours doesn't, read our list of [what's normal and what's a red flag in an ATM agreement](atm-placement-agreement-red-flags.html).

## What should you say to the customer right now?

Stay calm and keep it short. Here is a simple script.

1. "I'm sorry that happened. I can't open the machine or refund cash from my register."
2. "Call the number on the front of the ATM. That's the company that owns it."
3. "Also call the number on the back of your card. Your bank opens the claim."
4. "Keep your receipt, if you have one, and note the time."

The CFPB gives customers the same advice: tell your bank, and call the machine's owner if your bank doesn't own it.

Don't give cash from your drawer. You'd be paying for something you can't verify, and you'd be the one out the money if the machine did pay out.

## What happens after the customer files a claim?

This part happens behind the scenes. You will probably never see it.

1. **The bank opens a claim.** Federal rules treat an ATM withdrawal as an electronic transfer, so the customer has legal error rights.
2. **The claim travels to the owner of the machine's cash.** Industry guides say the owner has a short window, often about ten days, to send proof.
3. **The proof is the journal.** It's the machine's own record of the transaction. It shows whether the cash was counted, moved, and presented.
4. **The journal decides.** If it shows a failure, the customer is refunded. If it shows the cash went out, the claim is denied.

One more detail matters a great deal. An industry processing guide says that if the cash owner can't provide the right records in time, the customer gets a full refund plus the fee, and the networks add a charge. That's why it matters who is handling your machine.

## How long does an ATM dispute take?

For the customer, usually a couple of weeks, sometimes longer.

Under Regulation E, a bank generally has 10 business days to look into a reported error. If it needs more time, it must give the customer a temporary credit, and it can take up to 45 days to finish. Banks also have one more business day to fix an error once they confirm it.

Most customers hear back well inside that window.

## Does your store lose money on an ATM dispute?

If you host the machine and don't own the cash, usually no. A refund comes out of the cash owner's side, not your register.

The picture changes if you own the ATM and load your own cash. Then the claims land on you. You'd need to pull the journal, send it on time, and keep your own records. If that sounds like a lot, it's one reason some owners hand off the cash and processing side.

## How do you cut down on ATM disputes?

Most claims come from a few causes you can reduce.

- **Jams and mis-feeds.** Worn bills and a dirty machine cause these. Careful loading helps.
- **A dropped connection mid-transaction.** A shaky connection can cut a withdrawal off at the worst moment. See [what an out-of-order ATM really costs](atm-out-of-order-cost.html).
- **Left-behind cash.** Many ATMs pull cash back if no one takes it. Customers who walk away early cause some claims.
- **No clear contact number.** If the customer can't find who to call, they end up at your counter.

## The bottom line

When a customer says the ATM kept their money, you don't have to decide who is right. Point them to the owner's number and their own bank. Let the machine's records settle it.

If you don't know who handles claims on your machine, find out today, before the first one walks up to your counter.
