It's the middle of November. Your accountant says you'll owe more than you hoped. Someone tells you to "buy some equipment before the end of the year."

You've been thinking about an ATM anyway. So you wonder: can I write it off?

Often, yes. But a lot of owners get one detail wrong, and it can push the whole deduction into next year. The date that counts isn't the day you pay. It's the day the machine is ready to use.

Here's how it works, in plain words.

## Can you write off an ATM machine?

An ATM you buy and use for your business is equipment. The IRS lets businesses recover the cost of equipment through depreciation. Normally, that means spreading the cost over several years.

Two rules let you deduct more of it right away:

- **Section 179.** You can choose to deduct the cost of qualifying equipment in the year you start using it, up to a yearly limit.
- **Bonus depreciation.** This lets you deduct a set percentage of the cost in the first year, on top of or instead of Section 179.

Your tax pro decides which one fits. The point is that an ATM usually qualifies as business equipment like any other.

## What changed with the 2025 tax law?

Bonus depreciation had been shrinking each year. The 2025 tax law, called the One, Big, Beautiful Bill, made 100% bonus depreciation permanent. It covers eligible property acquired after January 19, 2025. The IRS issued guidance on the change in January 2026.

The Section 179 limit went up, too. For tax years starting in 2026, the IRS set the limit at $2,560,000. It begins to shrink once a business places more than $4,090,000 of equipment in service in one year.

Most ATM buyers are nowhere near those numbers. For a small business buying one or a few machines, the limits are not the problem. Timing is.

## Does buying in December count for this year?

Only if the machine is in service by December 31.

The IRS uses the "placed in service" date. That's the date the equipment is ready and available for its use. IRS Publication 946 gives an example of a machine delivered one year but not installed and running until the next. It counts for the later year.

For an ATM, "ready" means more than a box in your back room. It means the machine is installed, programmed, connected, and able to hand out cash.

So if this year's deduction matters to you, don't wait for the last week of December. Give yourself room for a delay.

## Section 179 or bonus depreciation: what's the difference?

Both can let you deduct the full cost of an ATM in year one. They have different rules:

| | Section 179 | Bonus depreciation |
|---|---|---|
| First-year deduction | Up to the full cost, within the yearly limit | 100% for eligible property acquired after January 19, 2025 |
| Limited by your business income? | Yes | Ask your tax pro |
| Business use needed | More than 50% | Ask your tax pro |
| You choose it? | Yes, you elect it | It applies unless you opt out |

Two Section 179 rules matter for small owners. First, the deduction can't be more than your taxable business income for the year. Any extra carries forward. Second, the machine has to be used for business more than half the time.

## What counts as the cost of your ATM?

The IRS says your cost includes the price plus sales tax, freight, installation, and testing.

## What isn't a write-off?

This is where new owners get mixed up.

- **The cash you load.** It's still your money. It comes back to your bank account when customers withdraw it. We explain how in [how ATM owners get paid](how-atm-owners-get-paid.html).
- **A free placement ATM.** If an ATM company owns the machine in your store, there's no machine cost for you to write off. Your share of the fees is business income.
- **A leased ATM.** Leases follow different rules. If you're weighing one, read [should you lease an ATM?](should-you-lease-an-atm.html) and ask your tax pro.

Your surcharge income is taxable, too. The write-off lowers your tax. It doesn't make the machine free.

## How much does the write-off actually save?

Less than the sticker price. A deduction lowers your taxable income, not your tax bill dollar for dollar. Thomson Reuters makes this point about year-end equipment buys. Your savings depend on your tax rate.

Using the $2,730 example, say your combined tax rate were 25%, just to keep the math easy. Writing off the machine would lower your tax by about $680. You'd still spend $2,730.

There's also a payback rule. If the machine's business use drops to 50% or less in a later year, you may have to give back part of a Section 179 deduction. Selling the machine later can also bring some of the deduction back as income.

So buy an ATM because the location makes sense. The tax break is a bonus, not a reason. If you're not sure the location is busy enough, read [how much an ATM really costs](how-much-does-an-atm-cost.html) first.

## What should you ask your tax pro?

Bring these questions:

1. Should I use Section 179, bonus depreciation, or regular depreciation for this machine?
2. Does my business have enough income this year to use the deduction?
3. How does my Alabama return treat this?
4. What will happen if I sell the machine or move it to another location later?
5. How should I track my vault cash, so it isn't counted as income?

## The bottom line

An ATM you buy for your business can often be written off in the year you put it to work. But "put it to work" is the key. The deduction follows the install date, not the payment date. Order early, leave room for delays, and let your tax pro choose the method.
