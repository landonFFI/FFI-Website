Is an ATM Tax Deductible? What to Know Before You Buy One This Year

The short answer

Usually, yes. An ATM you buy and use in your business is equipment, so you can often deduct its full cost in the first year through Section 179 or bonus depreciation. The catch is timing: the machine has to be installed and ready to use by December 31 to count for this year. Confirm the details with your tax preparer.

It's November. You've been thinking about buying an ATM for your store, or adding one more machine to your route. Then someone tells you, "Buy it before the end of the year. You can write it off."

They're mostly right. But there's one detail that trips people up every year, and it can push your write-off into next year without you noticing.

Here's how the deduction works for an ATM, what changed recently, and what to do if you want it to count for 2026.

This is general information, not tax advice. Talk to your own tax preparer before you buy anything for the tax break.

Can you write off an ATM you buy for your business?

Usually, yes. An ATM is equipment, just like a cooler or a cash register. If you own it and use it in your business more than half the time, you can deduct what you paid for it.

The question is how fast. Normally, equipment is written off a piece at a time over several years. That's called depreciation. But two rules let many small businesses deduct the full cost in the first year instead:

  1. Section 179, which lets you choose to expense the equipment right away.
  2. Bonus depreciation, which lets you deduct a percentage of the cost in year one.

Your tax preparer picks which one to use. For most small owners buying one or two machines, either one can cover the whole price.

How does Section 179 work for an ATM?

Section 179 lets you treat the machine's cost as a business expense this year. For tax years that start in 2026, the IRS set the limit at $2,560,000 in its yearly inflation update. That's far more than any small ATM buyer will spend.

There's one limit that does matter for small businesses. The Section 179 deduction generally can't be bigger than your business income for the year. If your business made very little this year, Section 179 may not help much.

Here's a simple example. Say you buy a new Hyosung Halo II, which starts at $2,480 in our store as of November 2026. If you deduct the full $2,480 and your top federal tax rate were 22%, that would cut your federal tax by about $546. That rate is just to keep the math easy. Your own rate and savings will be different.

What changed with bonus depreciation?

This is the news part. Under the old rules, bonus depreciation was shrinking each year and was set to end after 2026.

The federal tax law passed in July 2025 changed that. It made 100% bonus depreciation permanent for qualifying property acquired after January 19, 2025. In January 2026, the IRS released Notice 2026-11 with interim rules on how it works, according to a summary from the accounting firm BDO.

Bonus depreciation doesn't have the business income limit that Section 179 does. So if your business had a slow year, your preparer may use bonus depreciation instead.

Used machines can qualify too, as long as you didn't use that machine before you bought it.

Why does December 31 matter so much?

This is the detail that trips people up.

The IRS doesn't count the equipment when you order it or pay for it. It counts it when it's placed in service. IRS Publication 946 explains this as the point when the property is ready and available for its specific use.

For an ATM, that generally means delivered, installed, and ready to take cards. A machine sitting in a box on December 31 usually doesn't count until next year.

So work backward from the date:

Step What to plan for
Order processed We place the order within 12 hours of payment
Shipping Delivery usually takes about two weeks
Delays Any delays usually come from the manufacturer
Setup Bolting down, programming, and a test withdrawal

Two weeks of shipping plus setup time adds up fast in December, especially around the holidays. If the tax year matters to you, don't wait until the week before Christmas to order.

Can you deduct the cash you put in the ATM?

No. This one surprises a lot of new owners.

The cash in the machine is still your money. It goes from your bank into the ATM, and settlement puts it back in your bank account after each withdrawal. It isn't spent, so it isn't an expense. We explain the money flow in how ATM owners get paid.

What you earn from the machine, the surcharge and any interchange, is income. The costs of running it, like processing, wireless service, and repairs, are usually deductible business expenses. Ask your preparer how to track them.

Does Alabama follow the same rules?

Not always. Alabama's tax code follows some federal rules and not others, and it can treat individuals and corporations differently. In late 2025, the Alabama Department of Revenue published an analysis of how the 2025 federal law lines up with Alabama's code.

The short version: your federal write-off and your Alabama write-off may not match. Your preparer should check both.

What should you ask your tax preparer?

Bring these questions:

  1. Should I use Section 179 or bonus depreciation for this machine?
  2. Is my business income high enough this year for Section 179 to help?
  3. What date does the machine need to be installed by?
  4. How does Alabama treat this on my state return?
  5. What records should I keep for the cost and the install date?

Keep your invoice, your shipping confirmation, and a note of the day the machine first went live.

The bottom line

An ATM you buy and use for your business is usually deductible, and often all in the first year. But the clock runs on the day it's ready to use, not the day you pay.
