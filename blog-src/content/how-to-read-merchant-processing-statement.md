You swipe, tap, and dip cards all day. At the end of the month, a statement shows up. It's long. The words are strange. The total at the bottom is less than you expected, and you're not sure why.

Most owners file it and move on. That's normal. It's also how small fees grow into big ones, because nobody is checking.

You don't need to be an expert to read a statement. You need one number and a short list of fees to question. Here's how.

## What is the one number to find on your statement?

It's your effective rate. This is the true cost of taking cards, with every fee counted.

Here's the math:

1. Find the total fees charged for the month. Count every line, not just the "discount rate" line.
2. Find your total card sales for the same month.
3. Divide fees by sales.

Say you paid $450 in fees on $15,000 in card sales. That's $450 divided by $15,000, which is 3%. This example is just for the math, not a typical rate.

Now compare it to the rate you were quoted when you signed up. If you were promised 2.1% and you're paying 3%, the gap is made of fees you weren't told about. Helcim, a processor that publishes a guide on this, makes the same point: the quoted rate and the effective rate often differ.

## What are the main parts of a processing statement?

Layouts differ. Most statements have the same four parts.

**Interchange.** This is the fee the card-issuing bank charges. It's set by Visa and Mastercard, and they publish the rates. It's the biggest part of your cost, and it's the same no matter who processes your cards.

**Card network assessments.** These are small fees paid to Visa and Mastercard. They are a fraction of a percent of each sale.

**Processor markup.** This is what your processor adds. It's usually a percentage plus a few cents per sale. This part is negotiable.

**Other fees.** Monthly fees, statement fees, batch fees, gateway fees, PCI fees, equipment leases. This is where most junk fees live.

If your statement doesn't separate these, that tells you something too. See the pricing section below.

## Which fees on a merchant statement are junk?

Not every extra fee is a trick. Some are real. Here's how to tell.

**Round-number fees.** Real network costs are rarely round. A flat $5.00 or $19.95 line with a vague name, like "regulatory fee" or "network access," is worth a call. Ask who receives it.

**PCI fees.** PCI is the card industry's security standard. Processors do pass along penalties when a business hasn't finished its yearly self-check. Merchant Maverick reports these fees often run about $20 to $100 a month. It's a red flag if the fee keeps coming after you finished the form, shows up right after you sign up, or is much higher than that.

**Statement and "paper" fees.** A fee for sending you the statement is hard to defend.

**Minimum fees.** Some processors charge extra if your fees for the month are below a set amount. It matters for slow months.

**Equipment leases.** A card terminal is not hard to find or expensive to own. A lease on one can cost far more over its full term. The FTC has charged sellers who pushed small businesses into multi-year terminal leases by wrongly saying their old machines were outdated. Look for a lease line you don't remember agreeing to.

**Fees that appear and grow.** Compare three months side by side. A new line, or a line that crept up, deserves a question.

## Are you on interchange-plus, flat-rate, or tiered pricing?

The statement shows which one you have.

| Pricing | How it looks | What to know |
|---|---|---|
| Interchange-plus | Interchange and markup on separate lines | Easiest to check. Often cheaper at higher volume. |
| Flat-rate | One percentage on every sale | Simple. The processor sets it high enough to cover the average cost. |
| Tiered | Sales sorted into "qualified," "mid-qualified," and "non-qualified" | Hardest to check. You can't see what a card network charged. |

Lightspeed's comparison says tiered pricing is usually the most expensive and the least clear. If your statement has "non-qualified" lines, ask why a sale landed there.

Flat-rate can be a fair choice for a very small business with few sales. It's worth switching to a statement you can read once your volume grows.

## What should you do after you find something odd?

Call your processor. Keep it calm and specific. Ask three things:

1. What is this fee for?
2. Who gets the money?
3. Where in my agreement is it written?

A real fee has a real answer. If the answer is "that's just our standard fee" and nothing more, ask for the page in your agreement. If you can't get one, you've learned something.

Also check how you can leave. Look for an early termination fee and any equipment lease before you decide anything. Our post on [what's normal and what's a red flag in an ATM agreement](atm-placement-agreement-red-flags.html) covers the same questions for ATMs, and many of them apply to card processing too.

## The bottom line

Find your effective rate. Divide total fees by total card sales. Then look for round-number fees, PCI fees, statement fees, and leases. Call and ask three questions on anything you don't understand.

It takes about ten minutes. It's worth doing every few months.
